Who will default, and what interest rate should they pay?

Predicting loan default and suggesting a risk-based interest rate, and measuring how much the answer depends on what the lender already knows about the applicant.

The two research questions

  1. Default: given what is known about a loan applicant, how likely are they to default? We predict a calibrated probability of default (PD) for every applicant and decline those above a cutoff.
  2. Interest rate: for applicants who are approved, what interest rate fits their risk?

Unit: one row = one loan applicant (one loan). Targets: loan_status (1 = defaulted, 0 = repaid) and loan_int_rate (% per year).

How a decision is made

applicant ──► model ──► probability of default (PD), e.g. 0.12
                              │
              PD ≥ cutoff ?  ─┤── yes ──► DECLINE (no rate offered)
                              │
                              └── no ───► APPROVE ──► rate model ──► suggested interest rate, e.g. 11.4%
  • Probability of default (PD): a calibrated probability from a Random Forest: among applicants given PD 0.30, about 30% actually default.
  • Approve / decline: decline when PD is at or above the cutoff. Each setting's cutoff was chosen on the training set as the one with the highest F1, then frozen: Reference 0.378, Pipeline A 0.367, Pipeline B 0.388. On the test set this approves about 83% of applicants.
  • Interest rate: only approved applicants get a suggested rate (Pipelines A and B; in the Reference the rate is already set). The rate model is a Random Forest regressor trained on past loans and the rates actually charged. It suggests the rate that similar past borrowers paid: in Pipeline A it uses the grade + applicant details, in Pipeline B the PD + applicant details (no grade). It learns from all past loans with a recorded rate, not only approved ones; the cutoff decides only whether a rate is offered. So it reproduces the lender's past pricing; it does not calculate a break-even or profit-maximising rate.
  • You can move the cutoff yourself and browse individual decisions on the Explore tab.

What is a loan grade?

Every applicant has a risk grade from A (safest) to G (riskiest). The grade is a combined evaluation of the applicant's credit history (for example past defaults and repayment record), summarised in one letter, much like a credit score such as CIBIL in India or FICO in the US. The grade then largely decides the interest rate (averages we calculated from the dataset's 29,315 cleaned loans that have a recorded rate):

Because it packs the whole credit history into one value, the grade is the most informative single input for the interest rate and adds a lot to default prediction. Like a credit score, we treat it as universal and available when someone applies. The dataset does not document how the grade was produced, so this is an assumption. Someone with no credit history yet (a newcomer) has no grade, which is the situation of Pipeline B.

Three settings: what is known at decision time?

The dataset contains each loan's grade (A–G) and interest rate. The rate is set by the lender and the grade exists only once the applicant has a credit history, so a model may only use them if they would really be known when the prediction is made. That is why we built three settings:

Why compare all three?

A lender meets applicants at different stages, and what is known about them differs. Each pipeline answers the question for one stage, and each has a clear role:

PipelineRole in this project
Pipeline A graded applicantMain pipeline for pricing. Grade + details → PD → approve/decline → interest rate. This assumes the grade is universal and available when someone applies, like a credit score (e.g. CIBIL) that any lender can look up, rather than a rating only this lender assigns after its own assessment. The dataset does not say how the grade was produced, so this is an assumption.
Pipeline B newcomerFallback for applicants without a grade (for example no credit history yet). It also shows how much the grade is worth: without it, default prediction and pricing are much weaker. To see whether we could get a higher score, we also tried predicting the grade first from the applicant's details and then the rate (a two-stage model). It did not increase the score (rate R² 0.303 vs 0.305 without it): the grade was guessed correctly only 49% of the time.
Reference already-priced loanUpper bound and model selection. With grade and rate both known it shows the best this data allows (e.g. for checking loans already on the books), and it is where all the models were first compared.

Why keep them separate rather than build one model:

  1. A model may only use what is known when the decision is made. The interest rate is decided by the lender, and a grade exists only for applicants with a credit history. Using them for an applicant who has no grade or has not been priced yet would be cheating (information leakage), and the results would look better than any real system could achieve. Each setting uses only what is truly available at its moment in the lending process.
  2. Each setting is a real situation a lender faces. Reference checking the risk of loans already on the books; Pipeline A pricing an applicant who has been graded; Pipeline B deciding on a brand-new applicant (the situation of our interactive tools and of a real application form).
  3. Predicting the rate must not use the rate. The interest-rate models are part of Pipelines A and B only, and their probability-of-default inputs come from models that never saw the rate; otherwise the answer would be hidden in the input.
  4. The gap between the settings is itself a finding. Comparing them measures how much the grade and the rate are worth: Reference vs A shows what the interest rate adds once the grade is known (almost nothing); A vs B shows what the grade adds (a lot: default F1 0.84 → 0.70, rate R² 0.91 → 0.31). Note that Pipeline A's rate accuracy (R² 0.91) reflects how this lender priced its loans from its grade; another lender may follow the grade less closely.
  5. The Reference setting is the upper bound. With everything known it shows the best performance this data allows, and it is where all the models were first compared before the choice was confirmed in A and B.

How to read these numbers

  • F1 (default, 0 to 1, higher is better): how well the model catches defaulters without wrongly declining good customers. It balances precision (of those declined, the share who really default) and recall (of all defaulters, the share caught).
  • Brier score (default, 0 to 1, lower is better): how accurate the predicted probability of default is, the average of (predicted probability − what happened)², where what happened is 1 for a default and 0 for repaid. A PD of 0.9 for someone who defaults is a small error (0.01); a PD of 0.1 for them is a large one (0.81). Guessing the average default rate (0.219) for everyone scores 0.171.
  • R² (interest rate, 0 to 1, higher is better): the share of the differences in interest rates between borrowers that the model explains. R² = 0.9 means 90% is explained; R² = 0 is no better than giving everyone the average rate.
  • ± pp (interest rate): the typical error of the suggested rate (RMSE) in percentage points, e.g. ±2.70 pp means a suggestion of 11% is typically off by about 2.7 points.
  • ROC-AUC (default, 0.5 to 1): how well risky applicants are ranked above safe ones; 0.5 is random.
Main finding. The grade, a one-letter summary of the applicant's credit history, carries almost all the useful information. Knowing only the grade (Pipeline A) predicts default as well as knowing grade and rate together, and explains 91% of the variation in rates. For a newcomer without a grade (Pipeline B), default prediction drops to F1 0.70 and only 30% of rate variation can be explained: the limit is the information available, not the choice of model.

Baselines for comparison: predicting "everyone repays" gives F1 = 0 (accuracy 78%); guessing the average default rate for everyone gives Brier 0.171; guessing the average interest rate gives an error of ±3.24 pp (R² = 0).
